## 1. When the v3 mock and your eye disagree, which wins?

**The question.** Two of the things you flagged are the mock's design, built deliberately and faithfully:

- **The header bar sits flush to the top** with no gap above it. The mock's §2 header is a 48px bar hard against the top of the main area with a hairline under it, and all five converted screens now match. *(Your other point here was already true, for what it's worth — the title and the header actions are both centred on the same line, to within half a pixel.)*
- **"Food cost trend" is not in a card.** In v3 only tables and lists get a bordered container; the trend is drawn bare on the page. The mock's own markup is a plain `<section>` with no border, no fill, no band. So the page reads card / not-card / card because the mock does.

**Why it matters now.** The fold-in rule is "the mock is truth for structure; where it disagrees with the old app, the mock wins". That rule is what has kept five screens consistent. If your eye overrules it, the rule needs restating so the next four screens are built the new way rather than being corrected afterwards.

**Options**

- **A. The mock wins. Close both.** Costs: the page keeps a mixed rhythm and the header stays flush. Commits you to accepting that the v3 look is the target, including the parts that read oddly at first.
- **B. Your eye wins on both.** Costs: the flush header becomes ~24px of clearance on all five converted screens, and every dashboard section becomes a card. Both are one change each, but they are deviations that F7–F10 then have to copy, and the mock stops being the reference.
- **C. Split — keep the flush header, put the trend in a card.** Costs: the smaller change. Commits you to case-by-case judgement, which is slower per screen but keeps the ones you actively dislike from shipping four more times.

**My recommendation: A.** You are two screens into looking at a design you approved as a package, and the things that read oddly on first sight are the things that make it coherent once all ten screens match. Deviating now means deviating four more times.

**What would change it.** If either bothers you *after* a week of daily use rather than on first sight, that is a different signal and worth C. First-look reactions to a new layout are usually about unfamiliarity; week-two reactions are about the design.

## 2. Should the header's dividing line be wider than the content, or the same width?

There is also an older queue item saying the opposite: that the rule is currently **too narrow** and should run the full width of the screen, edge to edge, because that is what the mock does.

Both cannot ship. One of the two items has to close.

**Why it matters now.** It affects every screen, and whichever way it goes should be settled before F7–F10 build four more headers.

**Options**

- **A. Full-bleed — the rule runs the whole width, wider than it is now.** Costs: the rule visibly overhangs the content on a wide screen, which is the thing that caught your eye, only more so. This is what the mock draws.
- **B. Same width — the rule matches the cards exactly.** Costs: departs from the mock; the header stops reading as a band across the screen and starts reading as part of the content column.

**My recommendation: A**, but weakly, and only if you answered A to question 1. The mock's header is a band across the app, not a lid on the content column, and the overhang is what makes it read that way. If you answered B or C to question 1, answer B here — they are the same instinct and should not disagree.

**What would change it.** Whether you read the rule as "the bottom of the header bar" or "the top of the content". If it is the latter, B is right and the mock is wrong for this app.

## 3. Should "Last change" say "steady", or show nothing?

**The question.** On the Products screen, the Last change column prints the word **"steady"** on every product whose price has not moved. You saw fifteen of them in a row.

The mock prints "steady" too — but the mock's sample data only ever shows three of them at once, so it never shows you what fifteen looks like.

**Why it matters now.** Most of your catalogue has not moved recently, so most rows say "steady" most of the time. The column is carrying almost no information as it stands.

**Options**

- **A. Leave it as "steady".** Costs: a column of repeated words. Matches the mock and the Ingredients screen, which uses the same wording.
- **B. Blank it — show nothing when nothing changed.** Costs: a column that is empty most of the time, which can read as broken rather than as calm. Applies to Ingredients too.
- **C. Show a dash (—).** Costs: same as B but explicitly says "no value" rather than looking unfinished. Still a repeated mark, just a quieter one.

**My recommendation: C.** It keeps the column obviously alive without spending a word on it, and a dash is what every other "nothing here" cell in the app already uses, so it is the consistent choice rather than a new one.

**What would change it.** If you actually read "steady" as reassuring — "I checked, and it has not moved" — then A is right and this is not a defect at all.

## 4. Should the big Dashboard numbers be coloured?

**The question.** On the Dashboard's top strip, "Average food cost" and "Plates over target" render **red** when you are over target; "Not costed or priced" stays neutral. You noted that this makes the number itself carry a verdict.

**Why it matters now.** It is the first thing you see on the screen you open first.

**Options**

- **A. Keep it.** Costs: a number that is also a judgement. Matches the mock, and matches the chart, the sparklines and the menu rows, which are all coloured against target.
- **B. Make the numbers neutral, and put the verdict in a separate mark beside them.** Costs: a second element per cell; and the obvious candidate for that mark is a "vs last month" delta, which you deleted in v98 and declined again on 9 Aug.

**My recommendation: A.** Every other figure on this screen is coloured against your target, and that consistency is the whole reason the colour means anything. A neutral headline beside a red chart would be the odd one out, not the calm one.

**What would change it.** If the red is reading as alarm rather than as information — if opening the Dashboard feels like being told off — that is worth fixing, and B is the way.

## 5. Three different accent colours appear in the trend section. Reduce to two?

**The question.** In the Food cost trend section you can see, at once: the **chart line** (green when under target, red when over), the **orange ring markers** showing changes you made, and the **orange active pill** on the range control (3M). Three hues in one block.

**Why it matters now.** The markers and the range control are both orange, but they mean completely different things — one is your own history, one is a button.

**Options**

- **A. Leave all three.** Costs: a slightly busy section. Nothing is actually ambiguous.
- **B. Restyle the range control so the active option is not orange** — a plain tinted background instead. Costs: the active range is slightly less obvious. The markers stay orange, which is right: they mean "you did this", and the line means "here is where you stand", so they must not share a colour.
- **C. Restyle the markers instead, leaving the control orange.** Costs: markers would have to be neutral grey or share the line's colour, and sharing the line's colour would make "you made a change" look like a cost verdict.

**My recommendation: B.** The range control is a button, not data. It is the only one of the three that is not telling you something about your food cost, so it is the one that should stop competing.

**What would change it.** If you use the range buttons often enough that a quieter active state would slow you down, A is fine and the busyness is a fair price.
